Write ONE single self-contained HTML file: a self-playing 3D FOOSBALL (table football) GAME that runs the moment the file is opened.

WORK QUICKLY - DO NOT OVERTHINK
Plan briefly, then write the file. Pick ONE approach and commit to it. Do not weigh alternative rendering strategies at length, do not re-derive the maths several ways, do not draft the program repeatedly before writing it, and do not second-guess decisions you have already made. A clear, complete, working file written promptly is the goal; an exhaustively deliberated one is not. Spend your effort on the code, not on deliberation, and begin writing the file early.

IT MUST LOOK GENUINELY 3D
The failure mode to avoid above all else is "2.5D" - a flat board seen from above with shapes sliding on it.
- Use a real PERSPECTIVE projection with a stated field of view of about 50 degrees. Divide by depth. Do NOT use an orthographic, isometric, axonometric or dimetric projection, and do NOT fake depth by merely squashing the scene vertically - those all produce the flat look being rejected.
- STRONG FORESHORTENING is required: an object at the far end must be drawn noticeably smaller than an identical object at the near end. Screen-size must fall off with distance every frame, computed from the projection - never a constant radius.

CAMERA - A RAISED THREE-QUARTER VIEW THAT SLOWLY ORBITS
- Place the camera HIGH and OFF ONE CORNER, looking down at the foosball table from about 35 to 45 degrees above the plane of the pitch. This is an elevated three-quarter view, NOT a low view from behind the near edge and NOT a top-down one.
- Aim it at the centre. The foosball table must lie DIAGONALLY across the frame, so that one long side and one short side are both clearly visible and the pitch reads as a receding parallelogram.
- THE WHOLE OF THE FOOSBALL TABLE MUST BE INSIDE THE FRAME AT ALL TIMES, with a clear margin of empty room on every side. Nothing may ever be cut off by the edge of the canvas. Choose the camera distance so it occupies roughly 70 to 80 percent of the frame, and verify it still fits at every point in the orbit - not just at the start.
- SLOWLY ORBIT THE CAMERA ALL THE WAY AROUND: a continuous 360 degree yaw at a leisurely pace, one full revolution taking about 45 to 60 seconds, always keeping the same 35 to 45 degree elevation and always framing the whole scene. The motion must be smooth and constant, never jerky and never snapping back. Motion parallax - near objects sweeping across the frame faster than far ones - is the single strongest proof of real 3D.
- Because the camera goes all the way round, the scene must be correct from EVERY angle: depth sorting has to be recomputed each frame from the current camera position, and nothing may be hard-coded to look right from only one side.

EVERYTHING IS SOLID - NOTHING MAY BE SEE-THROUGH
Rounded objects must not appear as hollow rings with the floor visible through them, and no dark upright slabs may stand on the playing surface. Obey all of the following.
- EVERY object is fully OPAQUE. Set globalAlpha back to 1 before drawing any solid geometry, and never leave it lowered after drawing a shadow or highlight. If you are on WebGL, disable blending for solid geometry and enable the depth test.
- Every rounded object is a FILLED shape. Fill it, then stroke it if you want an outline - never stroke alone. A circle that is only stroked is a transparent ring and is exactly the bug being described. The playing surface must never be visible through an object, and no object may ever be visible through another object.
- The walls, rails, frame, body, legs and floor are all filled solid too. No wireframes, no outline-only shapes, no unfilled paths anywhere in the render.
- Use alpha BELOW 1 for exactly three things and nothing else: soft contact shadows, light falloff on the surface, and any deliberate glow. Everything else is fully opaque.
- NOTHING UNEXPECTED STANDS UP OUT OF THE PLAYING SURFACE. The only things above it are the pieces named in the scene description below. Do not leave stray rectangles, panels or slabs standing on the surface as a side effect of drawing something else. If drawing an inner wall or a recess risks putting a shape on top of the surface, draw it as a dark filled ellipse with a light rim instead.
- Draw strictly back-to-front by depth (or use the depth buffer). A nearer object overlaps a farther one; near walls overlap objects behind them.

DRAW ORDER - PIECES MUST NEVER VANISH
Moving pieces must never vanish while only their grey shadows remain on the surface. That is a depth-sorting mistake and it is fatal. Prevent it exactly like this:
- Draw the STATIC scene FIRST, in this order: room and floor, then the body/cabinet, then the playing surface itself, then the markings painted on that surface.
- THEN draw the contact shadows.
- THEN draw the moving pieces, depth-sorted ONLY AMONG THEMSELVES.
- NEVER put the playing surface, the floor or the cabinet into the same depth sort as the pieces that rest on them. A piece resting on the surface is always drawn after it. If a piece is ever hidden behind the surface it is standing on, your sort is wrong.
- EVERY moving piece must be drawn on EVERY frame, without exception. There is no condition under which a piece that is in play is skipped. Do not cull anything by depth, by facing direction, by a near/far plane test, or by any other visibility test. If you compute a depth value for sorting, make sure a NaN or a negative value can never cause a piece to be dropped - if in doubt, draw it.
- A frame in which a shadow appears with no object above it is a bug. Shadows and their objects are drawn from the same position in the same frame.

THE SCENE
- A FOOSBALL table standing in a dark room. The PITCH is a green playing surface with white markings - a halfway line, a centre circle, and a marked goal area at each end - enclosed by solid wooden side walls with a lit top edge and a visible woodgrain running along them. The cabinet below has real thickness with four legs reaching the floor.
- EIGHT ROUND STEEL RODS run across the width of the table, spaced along its length, each passing through both side walls and sticking out with a handle on one side. The rods are polished chrome with a bright specular streak along their length, and each handle is a ribbed grip in the team colour with visible ridges, not a plain cylinder. Four rods belong to the red team and four to the blue team, interleaved in the standard order: goalkeeper, two defenders, five midfielders, three attackers - for each side, facing each other.
- ON EACH ROD SIT ITS PLAYER FIGURES, AND THEY MUST BE PROPERLY MODELLED LITTLE MEN - NOT BLOBS. Each player must read as a footballer rather than a featureless plastic peg. Build every figure from these distinct parts, each visible as its own shaded form:
    * a HEAD: a small sphere sitting clear of the shoulders, in a skin tone, with a darker cap of hair on top
    * a TORSO: a tapered body, wider at the shoulders than the waist, wearing the team colour
    * TWO ARMS held out from the body, angled slightly away from the torso so daylight shows between arm and chest
    * TWO LEGS descending from the hips, with a visible gap between them, ending in small dark BOOTS
    * one leg is the KICKING LEG, planted forward of the other so the figure reads as mid-stride rather than standing to attention
- The boots must come down to just above the pitch surface, with a narrow visible gap - roughly the ball's radius - between boot and cloth. A figure whose body simply stops in mid-air above the pitch looks broken.
- Give each figure a JERSEY DETAIL: a contrasting horizontal band or a white collar and a shirt number across the chest, so the players read as footballers in kit rather than as plain coloured lumps. Red team in red shirts with white trim, blue team in blue shirts with white trim.
- Each figure is lit as a solid 3D object - a highlight on the head and shoulders, a darker side away from the light - and casts its own soft contact shadow on the pitch below it.
- The figures must be BIG enough to read: a player stands roughly two and a half ball-diameters tall. If a viewer cannot tell head from body at a glance, they are too small or too crude.
- At the centre of each short end is a GOAL MOUTH: a rectangular opening in the end wall, darker inside than the pitch.
- ONE BALL: a small light-coloured sphere with a specular highlight and a contact shadow, clearly rolling on the pitch.

HARD TECHNICAL RULES
- Everything (HTML, CSS and JavaScript) must live inside that one file. No external dependencies, no CDN links, no libraries, no frameworks - in particular NO three.js, NO cannon.js, NO physics engine of any kind. No web fonts, no external images or audio, no network requests. It must run correctly by double-clicking the file with no internet connection.
- You may use WebGL directly, or draw the 3D yourself with the Canvas 2D API doing your own projection and your own depth sorting. Either is acceptable. No image assets - every texture, if you use any, must be generated procedurally in code.
- Get the drawing context with canvas.getContext('webgl') or canvas.getContext('2d') - lowercase.
- The canvas fills the entire viewport (100vw by 100vh, no scrollbars, no margins) and correctly re-sizes with the window.
- It runs in a roughly SQUARE pane, about 900 by 900 CSS pixels. Compose for square. All HUD text must be at least 20 CSS pixels tall.
- Target 60fps with requestAnimationFrame and delta-time based motion. The requestAnimationFrame call that continues the loop must be INSIDE the draw function and must run every frame including after any early return, so the animation can never stop after the first frame.

PLAY - IT MUST PLAY ITSELF
- No human input is required and none is read: do NOT attach any keyboard, mouse or touch listeners. Two computer teams play each other forever and the game loops seamlessly.
- Each rod has exactly TWO degrees of freedom and no others: it SLIDES along its own axis across the width of the table, within limits, and it SPINS about that axis. Its position along the length of the table is fixed. This constraint is the whole character of foosball and must be respected exactly - a figure can never leave its rod, and a rod can never move up or down the pitch.
- A simple AI slides each rod so its nearest figure lines up with the ball when the ball is in that rod's band of the pitch, then SPINS the rod sharply forward to kick. The kick is a rotation over a few frames; the ball is launched by the impact of a moving figure, never by a scripted velocity.
- Give each AI a small reaction delay and aiming error so play is varied and scrappy, with deflections and rebounds off the walls and off other figures.
- When the ball passes fully through a goal mouth, increment that team's score, briefly pause, then drop a new ball at the centre with a small random push and play on.

PHYSICS - REAL, NOT SCRIPTED
- Every moving piece has position, velocity and mass. Nothing is keyframed; every position on screen comes out of the integrator.
- Friction decelerates every moving piece at a constant rate until it stops.
- Collisions resolve as impacts along the line of centres, conserving momentum. Two solid objects must never overlap or pass through each other.
- Wall rebounds leave at the mirrored angle with restitution under 1. Nothing may ever pass through a wall or escape the playing area.
- NUMERIC GUARD: clamp the frame delta to a maximum of 0.033 seconds before integrating, and clamp every object's speed to a stated maximum, so nothing can travel far enough in one frame to tunnel through a wall or another object.
- The simulation must never gain energy: if speeds rise over time rather than falling, it is broken.
- NEVER give an object a random velocity to make the scene look active. If it moves, an impulse moved it.
- The ball rolls with moderate friction and must rebound off the side walls, the end walls and the player figures.
- Figures are solid obstacles even when not kicking: the ball bounces off a stationary figure's body.
- USE THE WHOLE PITCH. Play must travel end to end, not stay stuck in one band.

CONTAINMENT AND ANTI-STALL - THE SCENE MUST NEVER LOCK UP
A moving piece must never pass through a wall, wedge outside the playing surface or stall the scene while only the camera moves. Enforce containment in this exact, literal way:
- Define the legal area ONCE as explicit numbers, for example halfWidth and halfLength, and give every piece a radius.
- At the END of every integration step, run an UNCONDITIONAL clamp - a real line of code that executes every frame, not a branch that might be skipped:
      p.x = Math.max(-halfWidth  + p.r, Math.min(halfWidth  - p.r, p.x));
      p.z = Math.max(-halfLength + p.r, Math.min(halfLength - p.r, p.z));
  and when a clamp actually bites, also reflect that component of the velocity and damp it. Reflecting the velocity WITHOUT correcting the position is what lets a piece embed itself in a wall - you must do both, in the same frame.
- If a piece is legitimately allowed to leave through a goal or a hole, test for that FIRST, handle the score, and reset the piece. Everywhere else the clamp applies with no exception.
- A piece must NEVER be drawn resting on top of a wall, on a rail, or beyond the edge of the playing surface. If you ever detect that state, put the piece back at its start position that same frame. Do not wait for a timer to notice.
- A PIECE MUST NEVER SIMPLY CEASE TO EXIST. Every piece that belongs in play is on the surface and visible in every frame, except for the brief moment it is legitimately passing through a goal or hole and the reset immediately after. If a piece is not on the surface and no scoring event is in progress, put it back at its start position that same frame.
- WATCHDOG: keep a timer of how long it has been since anything meaningful changed - a score, a reset, or a fresh strike. If NOTHING meaningful has happened for 6 seconds, force a full reset to the starting position and resume play. Also track total movement: if the sum of all piece speeds stays near zero for 1.5 seconds, re-serve immediately.
- The clip a viewer sees is only 11 seconds long. Any stall over 3 seconds ruins it. Visible action must be continuous from the first frame to the last.

NEVER-STUCK RULE
- If the ball's speed stays near zero for more than 1.5 seconds, have the nearest rod spin to free it. If 20 seconds pass without a goal, nudge the ball towards a goal. There is never a still frame and never a terminal state.

PRE-EMPT THESE CATASTROPHIC FAILURES
- Nothing may ever be drawn outside the playing area or leave the canvas.
- Do NOT let the scene stop when everything comes to rest - the reset rule must always fire.
- Do NOT draw any panel or overlay across the middle of the playing area.
- If you use WebGL, you MUST check that shaders compile and the program links, and fall back to something visible rather than a black canvas if they do not. A black pane is the worst possible outcome.

HUD
- A scoreboard readout at 20 CSS pixels or larger showing RED and BLUE scores, placed clear of the table. That is the whole HUD.

PALETTE
- Viewed small, dark-on-dark reads as an empty rectangle. Use a mid-tone, high-contrast palette where the moving pieces separate from the background in LIGHTNESS, not only in hue. A green pitch with crisp white lines, warm wood side walls, bright red and bright blue player figures that separate strongly from the green, steel rods with a metallic highlight, and a near-white ball.
- Behind the scene, a simple dark room with a visible floor-to-wall horizon line so the whole thing sits in a space rather than floating on a flat colour.

FINAL SELF-CHECK before you finish - confirm all eight:
1. The whole scene is inside the frame with margin, at every point of the 360 degree orbit.
2. Nothing on screen is see-through: no hollow rings, no wireframes, no background visible through a solid object.
3. Nothing unexpected stands upright on the playing surface.
4. A still frame could never be mistaken for a top-down 2D diagram.
5. Every moving piece is drawn on every single frame - never a shadow with nothing above it.
6. Nothing can escape the playing area, and the watchdog guarantees the scene never stalls.
7. The subject fills roughly 70 to 80 percent of the frame - not a small object adrift in empty space.
8. Every figure stays rigidly on its rod, and no rod ever moves along the length of the pitch.

CODE RULES - THE FILE MUST PARSE
A single unbalanced bracket can make the ENTIRE pane render blank: a JavaScript parse error kills the whole script element, so nothing runs at all - not the animation, not even the error-handling fallback. One stray character can destroy the whole submission. Take this seriously:
- Before you finish, RE-READ the complete script you have written from top to bottom and check that every bracket, parenthesis and brace opens and closes in the right order. Pay particular attention to long nested calls with array literals and ternaries inside them.
- Prefer SIMPLE, FLAT statements over deeply nested one-liners. Assign intermediate values to named variables instead of nesting four levels of brackets inside a single call. Shorter, plainer lines are much harder to get wrong, and clarity is worth more here than brevity.
- Declare every variable before any line that reads it, never reference a let or const above its declaration, never reassign a const, bounds-check every array index, and iterate backwards when removing items from an array mid-loop.
- Wrap the body of your animation frame in try/catch so one bad frame cannot stop the loop - but understand that this does NOT protect you from a parse error, which happens before any of your code runs. Only correct syntax protects you from that.
